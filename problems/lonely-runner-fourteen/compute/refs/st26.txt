                                           it has been reinterpreted in the context of view obstruction and billiard trajectories [5], covering

3.2. Backward projection. Backward projection is just the application of πp to pull back the
speed tuples to Zkp . Just as lifting can reduce the size of the search space, projection achieves
a similar effect. This is due to the fact that for any set S,
                                                   |πp S| ≤ |S| .
The only time projecting becomes counterproductive is if we lift afterwards with the same
constant c that was previously used, because then we are intersecting with the same improper
set and hence no speed tuples can be further eliminated.
   Therefore, in general, we can lift with c = 2 successively, then project back, then lift with
c = 3 successively and project back, and so on. This is the strategy that we use in our verification
algorithms in Subsection 5.2.

                               4. Eventual properness of (1, 2, . . . , k)
    According to Remark 3.2, when k + 1 is prime, we have to lift with the multiplier c = k + 1.
This lift is extremely computationally intensive: for k = 12, the c = k + 1 lift requires checking
(13/2)12 ≈ 5 · 109 times more tuples than the c = 2 lift (for fixed p and input set). If we
are to verify the conjecture for k ∈ {10, 12} in a reasonable time, we need to avoid doing this
lift computationally. For many primes p, after lifting I(10, p, 1) with c = 2 several times and
projecting back, the only tuples that cannot be shown eventually proper is, up to symmetry,
(1, 2, . . . , 10). The analogous observation holds when k = 12.

5.1. Equivalence of speed tuples. It is clear that some speed tuples in an ansatz are essen-

of the following actions:
      • Permuting the coordinates of u
      • Flipping the sign of a coordinate ui to −ui
      • Multiplying u by some a ∈ Z×   p.

equivalence indeed preserves eventual properness.

5.3. Further particulars. We implement the algorithms of Subsection 5.2 in C++. Compared
to the original implementations of [10] and [14], we improve practical performance by refac-
toring to support parallel execution and by using more memory-efficient data structures. The
development of our code was assisted by AI-based tools, but the process was closely supervised
and the code was manually verified. We also tested our implementation against that of [14] on
a number of smaller cases.
   In computing S1 , we modified the depth-first-search technique developed by [10] to account
for the equivalence relation mentioned in Subsection 5.1.
   These refinements dramatically improve runtime. We verified the case of k = 8 in 2 seconds
(15 minutes using [14]), k = 9 in 41 seconds (23 hours using [14]), and k = 10 in 45 minutes on
a 10-core Apple M4 processor (excluding compilation time). The proofs of k = 11 and k = 12
were run in multiple batches, with multiple versions, and on multiple machines, making the
exact time taken incomparable to other cases.
   A heuristic estimate from the smaller cases is that the time required to verify the emptiness
                                   (k+1)/2
of J(k, p) grows in proportion to p k2k . Interpolating this suggests that completing the proof
of k = 11 will take approximately 40 hours, and k = 12 approximately 40 days, on the same
10-core machine.
   The source of the computation, along with the log files and detailed descriptions, can be

                                   7. Concluding remarks
  In Section 4, we mentioned that, surprisingly, we only need to perform c = 2 lifts (more than
once) to eliminate all speed tuples apart from those whose projection modulo p is (1, 2, . . . , k),
                          ELEVEN, TWELVE, AND THIRTEEN LONELY RUNNERS                                        11

which is a tight speed tuple. From Goddyn and Wong [7], a speed tuple v ∈ Zk>0 is said to be
tight if the strict inequalities cannot simultaneously hold in the statement of Conjecture 1.2.
   An easy continuity argument shows that if v is not tight, then there is T and ϵ > 0 such
that every t ∈ (T, T + ϵ) is a witness time for v. Therefore, for any non-tight speed tuple v,
                                                                 1
there is L such that for any l ≥ L there is a witness time in lp   Z. Our observation suggests a
far stronger statement, that there is a universal L for every non-tight, coprime v.
Conjecture 7.1. Let k + 1 be a positive integer. There exists a constant D such that, for any
integer d ≥ D, every non-tight speed tuple v ∈ Zk>0 with gcd(v) = 1 has a witness time in d1 Z.
  On a different note, the algebraic method in Proposition 4.1 may be extensible to verify
Conjecture 1.2 for other classes of speed tuples.
  Looking ahead, the primary bottleneck in extending our results to k = 13 is the efficient
computation of I(k, p, 1). Progress here likely requires a better understanding of speed tuples
that do not have a witness time in an ansatz, which in turn could yield stronger pruning
conditions and make the initial sieve more tractable.
